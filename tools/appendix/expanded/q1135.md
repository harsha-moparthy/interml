**Potential-based reward shaping adds guidance while preserving optimal policies under specific assumptions.** Instead of giving arbitrary bonuses that may change the task, define a potential $\Phi(s)$ and add

$$F(s,a,s')=\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)$$

to the original reward. Use the same discount factor $\gamma$ as the original objective. [1][2]

Why this form? Over a trajectory of length $T$, the extra discounted return telescopes:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t[\gamma\Phi(s_{t+1})-\Phi(s_t)]
=-\Phi(s_0)+\gamma^T\Phi(s_T).$$

All interior terms cancel. For an infinite-horizon discounted problem with bounded potential and $\gamma<1$, the final term tends to zero. From a fixed starting state, every policy's return shifts by the same constant $-\Phi(s_0)$, so the ordering of policies is unchanged.

For a finite episode, ensure the terminal contribution vanishes or is otherwise policy-independent; setting terminal potentials to zero is a common construction. Ignoring that term can change which terminal state or episode length is preferred. For undiscounted or time-dependent settings, state the corresponding boundary assumptions rather than applying the discounted proof blindly.

As an intuition, a robot's potential could be negative estimated distance to its destination. Progress then provides a local learning signal, while the telescoping structure prevents arbitrary accumulated progress bonuses from redefining the objective. An arbitrary “one point whenever closer” rule lacks that guarantee and can invite cycles or unintended incentives.

**Policy invariance does not guarantee faster learning.** A poor potential can increase variance or complicate optimization, and function approximation introduces practical difficulties. Evaluate sample efficiency and final task return separately. The mathematical guarantee concerns the underlying reward transformation under its assumptions; it does not certify that a finite training run will discover the optimal policy. [1][2][4]
