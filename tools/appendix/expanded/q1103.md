**Next sentence prediction (NSP) is binary classification of a pair of text segments. It does not generate the next sentence.** Original BERT constructs about half its pairs from consecutive segments and half with a randomly sampled second segment. “Sentence” here can mean a span containing more than one natural-language sentence. [1][2]

For example, A = “The alarm rang.” and B = “Leena woke up.” might be a positive pair if adjacent in the source. A = “The alarm rang.” and B = “Granite contains quartz.” might be a negative pair. The label comes from the sampling process, not a human judgment of whether the two sentences could logically occur together.

The packed input is `[CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]`. A classifier uses the final representation of `[CLS]` (with the original pooled transformation) to produce two class logits. Softmax gives $p(IsNext\mid A,B)$, and cross entropy penalizes low probability for the sampled label. If a positive pair gets probability $0.9$, its loss is about $0.105$; at $0.1$, its loss is about $2.303$.

**The intended intuition** is to make the encoder learn relationships spanning segments. But a negative pair from a different document may be recognizable merely because its topic changes. High NSP accuracy therefore does not establish sophisticated discourse understanding. Randomly sampled negatives can occasionally even be plausible continuations. [1][4]

Contrast three targets: causal language modeling predicts the next token; NSP predicts whether B was sampled as A's continuation; ALBERT's sentence-order prediction distinguishes consecutive segments in their original order from the same pair swapped. The last comparison makes a topic shortcut less useful. RoBERTa showed that strong pretraining need not retain NSP under its training recipe. Treat these as objective choices with experimental evidence, not a universal rule that sentence-pair supervision always helps or always harms. [4][5]
