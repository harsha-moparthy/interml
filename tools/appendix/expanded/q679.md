**Dynamic pricing is a feedback system.** Price influences rider demand and driver behavior, which changes future supply and demand, which then feeds the next price decision. Treating each moment as an isolated prediction can create oscillation or unstable incentives.

**A simple failure story.** A local shortage raises price sharply. Riders cancel and drivers move into the area. The next update sees excess supply and cuts price sharply, causing drivers to leave and demand to return. Smoothing, rate limits, and explicit response modeling can reduce this back-and-forth, though they also slow adaptation.

Forecast demand and available supply by location/time, but account for movement between neighboring cells. A driver attracted to one area is no longer available elsewhere, so independent cell policies can conflict. The objective should state service goals such as fulfillment, wait time, and earnings alongside revenue.

**Historical correlations are not elasticities.** High prices often occur during high-demand periods, so observed price and demand can move together even if increasing price reduces demand causally. Use suitable experiments or credible causal designs to estimate response rather than reading a naive regression coefficient as an intervention effect. [3]

Enforce applicable product and jurisdictional constraints outside the model's learned score. Test adverse scenarios, spillovers, delayed response, and rollback. “Market clearing” is an idealized objective, not proof that a real platform with queues, travel delays, and uncertain behavior will instantly reach equilibrium.

**Technical details and references.**

Forecast available drivers and rider demand by location/time, but include movement between neighboring cells: independent cell forecasts ignore drivers repositioning in response to price. A policy chooses bounded price multipliers using estimated demand/supply response and service objectives such as wait time and fulfillment. Simply setting price proportional to demand/supply can oscillate.

Use recent events with event-time handling, calibrated uncertainty, and smoothing/hysteresis across time and adjacent cells. Account for dispatch delays, driver incentives, and the fact that price changes future demand. Enforce product and jurisdiction-specific constraints through a separate policy layer; the model should not infer those constraints from historical prices.

Historical price/outcome correlations are confounded by demand shocks. Estimate elasticity from suitable experiments or credible causal designs, and evaluate spatial/temporal spillovers. Simulate adverse cases, cap rates of change, and provide rollback to a stable baseline. Measure fulfillment, rider wait, driver earnings, cancellations, volatility, and distributional effects. “Clearing the market” is an objective under uncertainty, not a guarantee of an instantaneous equilibrium.

**Mathematical and practical explanation.** Estimate demand response as a causal quantity, such as an elasticity under a defined price intervention, rather than a regression correlation from demand-driven historical prices. Smoothing and rate limits help control oscillation in the policy loop. Evaluate spillovers across neighboring areas because drivers and riders can respond to prices outside their originally assigned region. [3](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook)
