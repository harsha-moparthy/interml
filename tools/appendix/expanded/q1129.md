**Fairness criteria ask different questions, so satisfying one does not imply satisfying another.** Let $A$ denote a group, $Y$ the true binary outcome, $\hat Y$ the decision, and $S$ a predicted risk score. Which criterion is appropriate depends on the decision and the meaning of its labels. [1]

Demographic parity requires equal positive-decision rates: $P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a)$ is the same across groups. Equalized odds requires equal true-positive and false-positive rates: $P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=y,A=a)$ is the same across groups for each $y$. Within-group calibration requires $P(Y=1\mid S=s,A=a)=s$ wherever that score is supported.

These condition on different information. Demographic parity compares decisions overall; equalized odds compares decisions among people with the same outcome; calibration compares observed outcomes among people assigned the same risk. A well-calibrated score can still produce unequal error rates at a chosen threshold. [1][2]

Here is a numerical illustration. Suppose two groups have base rates $0.1$ and $0.5$, and both receive decisions with TPR $0.8$ and FPR $0.2$. Their positive-decision rates are $0.1(0.8)+0.9(0.2)=0.26$ and $0.5(0.8)+0.5(0.2)=0.50$. Equalized odds holds in this construction, but demographic parity does not.

For unequal base rates and imperfect, nondegenerate predictors, standard sufficiency/calibration and separation conditions generally cannot all hold together. State the exact score-level or decision-level criterion rather than claiming every imaginable fairness metric is always incompatible. Perfect prediction and special degenerate cases require separate treatment. [1]

Finally, measurement matters: historical labels can encode past inequity, small subgroups produce uncertain estimates, and averages can hide intersections. Report sample sizes and uncertainty, examine the decision's actual harms, and choose a justified objective. A metric is a diagnostic and design constraint; it is not by itself a complete judgment of fairness. [3][4][5]
