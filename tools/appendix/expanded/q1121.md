**Compare two models on the same examples and analyze their paired differences.** Pairing removes variation caused merely by evaluating on different cases. Two independent confidence intervals are not a substitute for a confidence interval on the actual difference of interest. [1][2]

Suppose A and B are correct on 82% and 84% of 1,000 examples. The two-point gain alone does not tell you whether the improvement is stable. It matters whether B fixes a few errors without breaking much, or whether the models disagree widely and happen to have a small net difference.

For a metric such as accuracy or a task score, a paired bootstrap repeatedly resamples example indices with replacement. For each resample, calculate both models' metrics on those same indices and record $\Delta=M_B-M_A$. Quantiles of this distribution can form a bootstrap interval under suitable assumptions. Resampling the models independently discards the pairing.

For binary correctness, McNemar's test focuses on discordant pairs: $b$ examples where only A is correct and $c$ where only B is correct. Under the null of equal error probabilities, the two directions are symmetric. An exact binomial test on $b+c$ discordant cases is useful when counts are small; large-sample chi-square approximations require appropriate conditions. [1]

**Respect dependence.** If many examples come from one user or document, resample clusters rather than pretending every row is independent. If performance over future time is the question, a random bootstrap may not capture temporal shift. Also predefine important slices and account for multiple comparisons rather than searching hundreds of slices for one favorable result.

Finish with practical significance: a statistically detectable gain may be too small to justify doubling latency. Report the effect size, uncertainty, sample unit, and operational tradeoffs together. Evaluation should support a deployment decision, not merely produce a small p-value. [3][4][5]
