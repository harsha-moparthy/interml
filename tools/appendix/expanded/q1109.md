**A static embedding assigns one vector to a vocabulary entry; a contextual encoder assigns a representation to a token occurrence.** Thus the two appearances of *bank* in “river bank” and “bank loan” start with the same static word vector but can end with different contextual representations. [1][2]

Word2vec and GloVe learn from patterns of word co-occurrence. Their training procedures differ, but both commonly yield a lookup table: given a word identity, retrieve its vector. The table can express broad semantic relationships cheaply, yet one vector must mix the word's different usages.

A contextual encoder processes surrounding tokens. In notation, a static lookup is $e(w)$, whereas a contextual representation is $h_i=f_\theta(x_1,\ldots,x_T)_i$. The subscript matters: the representation belongs to position $i$ in this particular input. It may change if the neighboring text changes even though the token identity is unchanged.

This also separates **input embeddings** from **output representations**. BERT still begins with learned token embeddings. Its layers transform those inputs into contextual vectors. Saying BERT “does not use embeddings” confuses the lookup step with the later contextual computation. Subword tokenization also lets it represent many words by multiple vocabulary pieces rather than requiring one entry per full word. [2][4]

For retrieval, neither averaging static vectors nor taking an arbitrary contextual token vector guarantees a good sentence-similarity space. Pooling and the training objective matter: Sentence-BERT explicitly trains sentence representations for useful comparisons. Static embeddings remain attractive when memory, latency, or data constraints make a large encoder unsuitable. Choose by measured task quality and resource cost, and check whether your evaluation contains ambiguous or domain-specific words; those are precisely where a single lookup vector may hide important distinctions. [3][5]
