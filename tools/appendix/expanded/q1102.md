**Selection for prediction and replacement with `[MASK]` are different operations.** Original BERT selects about 15% of eligible token positions. Among those selected positions, 80% become `[MASK]`, 10% become a random vocabulary token, and 10% remain unchanged. Every selected position still contributes to the reconstruction loss. [1][2]

Imagine 1,000 eligible tokens. On average, 150 are prediction targets: 120 are visibly masked, 15 are randomly replaced, and 15 look unchanged. The other 850 provide context without direct MLM targets. These are expectations, not a guarantee that every short batch has exactly these counts. Special tokens and padding are normally excluded from target selection.

For “the pilot landed safely,” suppose *landed* is selected but randomly replaced by *apple*. The target remains *landed*. If it is selected and left unchanged, the target is still *landed*. If it was never selected, its target is ignored. A useful implementation sketch is:

```python
labels = original_tokens.clone()
labels[~selected_positions] = -100  # ignored by cross entropy
# Corrupt input_tokens separately; do not corrupt labels.
loss = cross_entropy(logits.flatten(0, 1), labels.flatten(),
                     ignore_index=-100)
```

The mixture reduces dependence on seeing a special mask token at prediction sites. It does not eliminate all train–test mismatch, and unchanged targets can be easy. Its practical purpose is to learn useful representations under a mixed corruption task. [1]

A common bug is `labels[input_tokens != MASK] = -100`: it silently discards the random-replacement and unchanged targets. Another is supervising the corrupted token rather than the original token. Finally, the MLM mask says **which tokens to predict**; the attention mask says **which positions may be attended to**, such as excluding padding. Confusing them changes the model. Dynamic masking resamples corruption across presentations, increasing the variety of training tasks for the same text. [3][4]
